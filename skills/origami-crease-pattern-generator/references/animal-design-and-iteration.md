# Animal design and iteration

Use this reference for original animals and creatures when recognition, allocation, physical shaping, or material behavior is a central part of the task. It turns a study of Rokishi's public creation notes and directly related design-method sources into reusable decisions. It does not authorize copying any pictured model, crease pattern, diagram, or folding sequence.

## 1. Define the design target before the crease pattern

Write down the intended subject, pose, strongest viewing angle, realism or stylization level, difficulty, reproducibility, and material assumptions. Then identify:

- three to six signature traits that distinguish the subject from nearby species or forms;
- the one silhouette or facial cue that must survive every simplification;
- broad surfaces that require paper area rather than another flap;
- which details may be shaped later instead of encoded structurally;
- the intended source of originality: subject reading, allocation, topology, shaping, pose, material, color change, or their combination.

Compare existing origami interpretations before designing. Record structural overlap and the planned point of differentiation. A familiar base can still support an original result, but do not describe a design as clean-sheet work when its allocation or topology substantially follows a known model.

Prototype an uncertain signature feature early. A convincing body cannot rescue a species-defining head, muzzle, horn, hump, ear, or nose that has never worked in paper.

## 2. Choose the entry route from the strongest known constraint

Do not force every model through the same design route.

| Route | Start here when | First evidence to seek |
|---|---|---|
| Signature-feature-first | one hard part carries identity | a paper prototype of that part with a connection flap left for the body |
| Known-base-first | a traditional or familiar base already supplies most limbs | a part map showing where the missing regions can be added or redistributed |
| Topology/allocation-first | a promising flap arrangement or line structure comes first | a candidate subject and a feature map that genuinely fit the available regions |
| Ratio-first | a constructible reference ratio is the main constraint | several allocations derived from the same ratio, not just one polished CP |
| Bottom-up geometry | useful constrained lines or molecules emerge locally | a viable boundary and sheet ratio calculated only after the structure is understood |

Switch routes when the evidence changes. Keep a connection flap or reserved region when developing a feature independently so that local success can still join the whole model.

## 3. Compare allocation candidates as paper economies

When the design is uncertain, compare at least two materially different allocations before polishing either one. For each candidate, record:

- named tips, surface regions, body junctions, and unused paper;
- effective flap length, including river or band regions that become part of a limb;
- flap width at the root and expected thickness at layer hot spots;
- which features use the paper edge and which are internal;
- where front/back color, fur, skin folds, or a clean body panel must appear;
- head, limb, tail, horn, and body proportions after collapse;
- access for shaping, locking, and separating paired features;
- whether back split, belly split, or a hybrid gives the required surfaces and edges;
- whether the candidate can be collapsed, repeated, and eventually diagrammed.

A symmetric final animal does not require symmetric paper allocation. Asymmetry can consume otherwise wasted diagonal regions, narrow a flap, or free paper for a high-priority feature. Judge it by the finished model and folding behavior, not by CP appearance alone.

Edge flaps offer color-change access and often more usable surface at a feature. Internal flaps can preserve body edges, reuse internal paper, or make sturdy limbs, but commonly add thickness and difficult layer access. Treat this as a tradeoff rather than a fixed rule.

## 4. Decide ratios without making geometry an aesthetic goal

There are three useful ratio workflows:

1. **Bottom-up:** extend constrained lines and intersections, understand the working structure, then choose the paper boundary and derive the reference ratio.
2. **CP-first top-down:** start from a target base or topology, solve its boundary, and then find a practical construction ratio.
3. **Ratio-first top-down:** choose a familiar ratio and explore multiple structures that it can support.

Prefer reference points a folder can construct, but do not replace a working irregular structure with a cleaner grid or all-22.5-degree version unless paper tests confirm that the change preserves access, collision clearance, and collapse behavior. Geometric tidiness is not evidence of practical foldability.

Matching the widths of added regions can simplify their connection to an existing structure. It can also make reference folding harder, so record why the match helps and test the actual sequence.

## 5. Select the tool by the unknown being tested

- Use Oriedita or a comparable editor for constrained angles, extended intersections, redraws, offset corrections, local flat-fold checks, and extracting a candidate reference ratio.
- Use a tree or box-pleating planner for flap connectivity, grid allocation, corner use, and stretching candidates.
- Use physical paper for surface width, collision, layer access, thickness, stiffness, accidental useful forms, and three-dimensional shaping.
- Use photographs from several views to test recognition, expression, body mass, and pose.

Digital and physical evidence answer different questions. A valid CP does not prove a good subject, and a beautiful one-off finish does not prove a repeatable collapse.

## 6. Run hypothesis-led iterations

Keep a ledger with one controlled question per iteration:

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| Hypothesis | the feature or failure mechanism being tested |
| Change | the geometry, allocation, paper, or shaping change |
| Expected effect | what should improve and what may get worse |
| Evidence | CP validation, paper behavior, photographs, or sequence trial |
| Decision | adopt, reject, revert, or retest |

Test in this order unless the model gives a stronger reason:

1. signature feature and subject recognition;
2. global allocation, proportions, and effective flap lengths;
3. layer access, collision, and thickness;
4. multi-view silhouette, expression, and body mass;
5. cosmetic shaping and finish quality.

Record rejected candidates and the reason: wasted paper, poor width, hidden feature, excess thickness, collision, unstable shaping, difficult references, or low reproducibility. A failed regularization or material choice is useful evidence when it prevents the same detour later.

## 7. Separate structure, shaping, and material

Classify every desired feature:

- **Structural:** needs a flap, surface, color boundary, body mass, or stable layer path.
- **Shaping:** can be produced by eyelids, lips, curves, gussets, crimps, spreading, or final three-dimensional work.
- **Material-dependent:** needs stiffness, thinness, texture, wet-folding response, foil backing, or reinforcement.

Do not encode all visible detail into the CP. A lower-resolution or simpler base can reduce thickness and leave more freedom for three-dimensional shaping. Conversely, a finish-heavy simple CP may require thin paper, reinforcement, or advanced shaping and should not be presented as easy merely because it collapses cleanly.

Choose paper before the final topology is frozen when layer count or surface character matters. Check caliper or thickness where possible, sheet size, stiffness, memory, texture, wet-folding behavior, glue compatibility, front/back appearance, and color-change needs. Material is a design constraint, not a final decoration.

## 8. Evaluate the model at separate claim levels

Use a final review that keeps these claims separate:

| Claim | Minimum evidence |
|---|---|
| Subject fidelity | named cues visible from the intended views and distinguishable from look-alike subjects |
| Paper allocation | feature map, proportions, surfaces, and major unused regions accounted for |
| CP validity | exact adopted geometry passes the declared local/global checks |
| Physical foldability | at least one paper trial reaches the intended state without undocumented intervention |
| Finish quality | photographs show the intended silhouette, expression, pose, and surface treatment |
| Reproducibility | repeated fold or an independent folder reproduces the critical features |
| Diagramability | references, layer access, and ordered operations can be communicated without relying on intuition alone |

Review front, profile, back, and three-quarter views when relevant. Small changes in eyelid direction, muzzle width, ear position, or body balance can change the perceived species or expression. External critique is evidence about a particular failure, not a universal rule; translate it into a testable claim.

Stop polishing a topology after several materially similar failures. Revert to the last working structure, switch the entry route, simplify a feature, change the material assumption, or report the limitation.

### Directly related methods freshly reviewed

- The image-led [ネコの創作過程](https://sukesanorigami.blog.jp/archives/23492108.html): feature sketches, local prototypes, integration, and material refinement.
- Hideo Komatsu's [非対称的モデルについて](https://origamiplans.hatenablog.jp/entry/20160224/1456322897): several forms of asymmetry, including symmetric subjects with asymmetric allocations.
- Hideo Komatsu's [小松風](https://origamiplans.hatenablog.jp/entry/20140419/1397910842): 22.5-degree, uniaxial, molecule, and back-split/belly-split structural vocabulary.
- Hideo Komatsu's [折り線トポロジー](https://origamiplans.hatenablog.jp/entry/2020/03/31/201707): separating crease connectivity and mountain-valley structure from one exact geometric embedding.
- [文系作家の行き当たりばったり創作法](https://origami-fantasia.com/272/): subject research, signature-part prototypes, a connection flap, and integration into a familiar allocation.
- The same site's [背割れと腹割れの比較](https://origami-fantasia.com/907/): similar silhouettes can require different mountain-valley assignments, surfaces, and head shaping.
- Kota Morisue's [私の創作を変えた2人の異邦人](https://note.com/dangerous_fold/n/nea941642e83a): bottom-up detail versus top-down whole-form constraints and the value of simple bases.
- Satoshi Kamiya's [領域の追加](https://www.folders.jp/uc/2021/790/) and [ヒダの幅を22.5度の構造と合わせる](https://www.folders.jp/uc/2021/813/): where to add a region, the thickness cost of axis additions, and width-matching tradeoffs.
- Abrashi Origami School's [Colour change – part 1](https://abrashiorigami.com/colour-change-part-1/): edge/perimeter constraints, completeness, corner placement, and iterative boundary design.
- [Box Pleating Studio](https://bp-studio.github.io/): box-pleating planning and stretching support for allocation exploration.
